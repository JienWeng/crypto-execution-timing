\noindent This supplement documents the exploratory extension, complete comparisons, proofs and related literature. Quantities called gains are paired reductions in a hypothetical execution objective, not realised trading profits.
\section{Chronology and reproducible design}
The initial study used Bitcoin in June, July and August 2026 for training, calibration and evaluation. Its results and peer-review reports existed before this extension was designed. The extension protocol was recorded before computing the expanded outcomes, but after inspecting that original result. It is therefore retrospective and exploratory, not preregistered or an independent confirmation. Bitcoin's August window overlaps the original analysis. All twelve asset--month windows, four forecast constructions and six cost scenarios were fixed in the extension protocol. No additional profile search or outcome-driven sample expansion was performed.

Public Binance monthly spot archives contain one-minute BTCUSDT and ETHUSDT bars for January--August 2026 \citep{binance2026}. All sixteen archive hashes matched their published checksums. Validation covered all 699,840 rows: twelve columns, finite entries, exact calendar boundaries, microsecond timestamps spaced by one minute, no duplicates or gaps, close-time consistency, positive prices, OHLC bounds, nonnegative volumes and integer trade counts, and taker volumes bounded by corresponding totals. Positional indexing occurred only after validation. The manifest records each archive URL, hash, row count and individual checks. An initial parser interface error was corrected before outcomes were produced; its history is recorded separately.

Let $i$ index the decision minute, at minute offsets $5,35,\ldots,1415$ each UTC day. If $V_k$ and $V_k^B$ are total and taker-buy base volume, the signal is
\[
 x_i=\frac{\sum_{k=i-5}^{i-1}(2V_k^B-V_k)}{\sum_{k=i-5}^{i-1}V_k},
\]
with zero assigned for a zero denominator. These five bars are completed before the decision. Execution prices are opens at $i+1,\ldots,i+15$, giving a one-minute initial delay. Returns in basis points use the fixed decision-minute open as denominator. There are 48 nonoverlapping execution episodes per day per asset. They may nevertheless be statistically dependent.

For each asset, fit fifteen regressions with intercept on the training month, one for each subsequent price horizon. If $\widehat\beta_j$ is the slope and $\bar x_{\rm train}$ the training signal mean, use $\mu_{ij}=\widehat\beta_j(x_i-\bar x_{\rm train})$. The unconditional fitted intercept is deliberately excluded. Training, calibration and evaluation use three consecutive months, starting with January, February and March. The window then advances one month at a time, ending with June, July and August. No evaluation observations enter that window's fit or calibration. Rolling windows share observations and regimes; twelve rows do not represent twelve independent experiments.

For each profile solve the nonnegative, unit-volume quadratic programme in the main text. The baseline minimises the same cost without price information. An equality-constrained solution is used when feasible; otherwise the original projected-gradient simplex solver is used. Maximum observed KKT residual was $1.29\times10^{-12}$. The exact parallel-profile invariance is also a numerical control: absolute pooled mean residuals were below $1.5\times10^{-14}$ basis points. Tables display their analytical zero. Code and protocol hashes were unchanged during the completed run.

\section{Complete empirical comparisons}
All forecast variants have identical final coordinates. Endpoint predictions, squared errors and directional-hit indicators coincide episode by episode, so aggregate RMSE, hit rate and $R^2$ coincide within each window. Endpoint $R^2$ is $1-\sum_i(p_{iN}-\mu_{iN})^2/\sum_i p_{iN}^2$, relative to a zero-return prediction. Hit rate compares forecast and realised terminal signs. The ramp is a restricted constructed profile; the mirror deliberately reverses temporal contrasts. Neither is represented as an independently trained competitor. The diagnostic holds terminal predictions fixed to expose omitted decision information.

\section{Proofs and economic interpretation}
\subsection{Forecast levels and endpoint insufficiency}
For any fixed-volume feasible set $\mathcal U\subset\{u:\mathbf1^\top u=1\}$,
\[
 K(u)-(\mu+a\mathbf1)^\top u=K(u)-\mu^\top u-a.
\]
The additive constant leaves the entire minimiser set unchanged. Likewise, for two feasible schedules $u,v$, $(u-v)^\top(p+a\mathbf1)=(u-v)^\top p$. This is price-level invariance, not invariance to a drift that changes prices differently across slots. Forecast-dependent fees, constraints or impact coefficients can invalidate the stated argument.

An endpoint $\mu_N$ leaves $N-1$ temporal contrasts unspecified. The two-slot example in the article makes that omission explicit. For $K=\|u\|^2$ and $u=(x,1-x)$, differentiating gives $4x-2-(\mu_1-\mu_2)=0$, clipped to $[0,1]$. With prices $(3,2)$, flat, correct-shape and opposite-shape forecasts yield the reported $0,0.125,-0.375$ gains. These are synthetic dimensionless values. Additional structural restrictions can link the endpoint to all intermediate prices; the example does not deny their usefulness.

\subsection{Benefit, baseline penalty and curvature}
Write $H=\nabla^2K=2\eta NI+2rL^\top L/N$, $q_0=\mathbf1-Lu_0$ and $\delta=u_1-u_0$. Direct expansion gives
\begin{align*}
 A&=\delta^\top p,\\
 B&=2\eta N u_0^\top\delta-\frac{2r}{N}q_0^\top L\delta,\\
 C&=\eta N\|\delta\|^2+\frac rN\|L\delta\|^2,\\
 J_p(u_0)-J_p(u_0+w\delta)&=w(A-B)-w^2C.
\end{align*}
Convex baseline optimality implies $B\geq0$ for every feasible direction to another schedule. On an interior simplex optimum, $\nabla K(u_0)$ is a multiple of $\mathbf1$, and $\mathbf1^\top\delta=0$ gives $B=0$. This argument concerns the same inventory-adjusted cost that defines the baseline. It need not give the same sign for monetary costs after dropping the inventory term.

Let $D_j=\sum_{k\leq j}\delta_k$ with $D_N=0$. Since $\delta_j=D_j-D_{j-1}$, summation by parts gives $A=-\sum_{j<N}D_j(p_{j+1}-p_j)$. Earlier sales have positive $D_j$ and benefit from subsequent declines. The price move before the first execution slot cancels because both schedules still hold the full order. This is an accounting identity, not evidence of causal market impact.

\subsection{Historical fitting and its limits}
For $C_i>0$, set $z_i=(A_i-B_i)/(2C_i)$. Then
\[
 G_i(w)=C_i z_i^2-C_i(w-z_i)^2.
\]
A common gain-maximising weight therefore minimises a curvature-weighted squared loss and equals the clipped aggregate ratio in the article. It is not the mean of clipped episode ratios. With $\eta>0$, $C_i=0$ implies $\delta_i=0$ and a zero gain contribution. The $z_i$ are ex-post targets, unavailable at decision time; only historical observations can be used for policy fitting. This identity supplies no generalisation or coverage theorem.

Schedule mixing also differs from reoptimising scaled forecasts when constraints bind. Take the two-slot cost above and $\mu=(6,2)$. The full solution is $(1,0)$ and the baseline $(1/2,1/2)$. At half exposure their mixture is $(3/4,1/4)$. The endpoint-preserving half-scaled forecast $(4,2)$ instead optimises at $(1,0)$. Reoptimisation and mixing can agree within an unchanged active set, but not generally across active-set changes.

\section{Technical corrections to the related preprint}
The predecessor \citep{lai2026} considers an unrestricted OU-signal execution model. Its kernel requires correction before that model is used as context. Let $\tau=T-t$, $\kappa=\sqrt{r/\eta}$, and let $\phi\geq0$ denote signal mean reversion. The regular solution of
\begin{equation}
 B_t-[\kappa\coth(\kappa(T-t))+\phi]B+1=0
\end{equation}
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
 B(t)=\int_t^T e^{-\phi(s-t)}
       \frac{\sinh(\kappa(T-s))}{\sinh(\kappa(T-t))}\,ds.
\end{equation}
An integrating factor $e^{\phi\tau}\sinh(\kappa\tau)$ proves the expression. At $\kappa=0$, the ratio is interpreted as $(T-s)/(T-t)$; near completion $B(T-\tau)=\tau/2+O(\tau^2)$. The inverted ratio printed in the predecessor has a reciprocal endpoint singularity and a divergent improper integral. It is not a valid numerical comparator.

The side convention also matters: $+fx$ is the waiting-cost drift term for acquisition, whereas liquidation uses $-fx$. A deterministic linear permanent-impact term can be removed from fixed-endpoint comparisons when it is a schedule-independent constant; this simplification does not extend automatically to transient or nonlinear impact. The unrestricted model allows signed trades and inventory crossings, unlike the empirical simplex problem.

A nonnegative-control Hamiltonian yields $\max(0,V_x/(2\eta))$ using the \emph{constrained} value function. Substituting the unrestricted quadratic value function does not solve the constrained problem. A deterministic acquisition example makes the distinction concrete. Set $\eta=Q=T=1$, $r=0$, and $f(t)=16e^{-0.1t}$. The buy-only optimum has
\begin{equation}
 v^*(t)=80(e^{-0.1t}-e^{-0.1z})\mathbf1_{\{t\leq z\}},
 \qquad \int_0^z v^*(t)\,dt=1.
\end{equation}
Convex first-order conditions establish this optimum. It completes at approximately $0.508529$ with cost $5.299888$. Corrected unrestricted feedback, clipped and stopped at zero inventory, completes at $0.260671$ with cost $5.811578$. These synthetic dimensionless values illustrate a constraint error, not market savings. Correcting the author's earlier expression and interpretation is a prerequisite for consistency, not the claimed contribution of this note.

The accompanying discrete OU verification permits unrestricted controls, uses the exact sampled OU transition, and charges a specified discrete interval exposure. That interval cost is a discrete-model assumption rather than an exact integral of every continuous-time cost component. Numerical checks of that model and of \eqref{eq:kernel} support the algebra within their stated assumptions. They neither identify the empirical BTC process as OU nor validate the hypothetical replay costs. In particular, an unbounded Gaussian adaptive signal does not generally admit a nonzero fixed exposure cap guaranteeing monotone trades on every sample path.
